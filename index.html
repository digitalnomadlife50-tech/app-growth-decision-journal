WIKIPEDIA APPS / READING CONTINUITY

Thread

Reading continuity in the Wikipedia apps
ArticleDiscussionReadView source

A first-person walkthrough of the evidence, questions, and trade-offs behind a proposed Continue reading feature.

Thread is a proposed internal codename for this independent decision record. It is not an approved internal project, a Wikimedia product, or an official Wikimedia initiative. The broader product concept is Reading continuity in the Wikipedia apps, and Continue reading is the proposed user-facing feature name. This journal follows my reasoning from the assignment to a six-week first release, the experiment I would run, and the questions I would still need to resolve.

Scope of this document: The assignment scenario and its figures are fictional. The supplied brief is the source for the facts and metrics below. The interpretation, hypothesis, and proposed decisions are mine. The four interview deliverables remain separate: (A) a scope and measurement plan, (B) a Phabricator epic and two user story tasks, (C) a Slack update to the Senior Director, and (D) a public reply to the volunteer editor.

The assignment [edit]

The quarterly goal is to increase the share of new app installs that return within seven days. Leadership is interested in a Continue reading feature and expects a first release within six weeks. I treated the feature idea as a starting hypothesis to examine against the evidence, while keeping seven-day return as the outcome.

The PM owns scope, delivery, and updates to the Senior Director while the manager is away. The exercise asks for a scope and measurement plan, a Phabricator epic and two complete stories, a leadership update, and a public reply to a volunteer editor. This journal is the supporting record of how I would make those decisions.

Two delivery constraints shape the work from the outset. Cross-device synchronization requires account work estimated to take at least a quarter, and the designer is unavailable for the first two weeks. A responsible first release has to fit those limits without lowering the bar for reader trust or measurement.

Starting evidence [edit]

The supplied 30-day snapshot contains three different kinds of signal. Return rates describe the outcome, incomplete-session rates describe reading behavior, and push opt-in rates describe permission for a channel. The incomplete-session and opt-in figures do not tell me whether a reminder would be useful or welcome.

MetricAndroidiOS
New installs returning within 7 days22%28%
Sessions ending partway through an article41%38%
Push notification opt-in rate61%34%

An incomplete article session does not establish that a reader intended to return. The reader may have been interrupted, found what they needed, or finished reading for the moment. I can use the behavior as a reason to investigate Reading continuity, but I cannot turn it into evidence of intent without more information.

Support separately reports that the app can lose a reader’s place when the app is backgrounded. An app review complains about notification volume. A volunteer editor asks whether the app stores what people read and where that data goes. I would treat these as signals to investigate, with different implications for reading friction, prompting, and trust.

The analyst warns that Android’s recent install trend was partly affected by a tracking change. A direct comparison with the prior month would mix a measurement change with a change in reader behavior, so I would establish a clean baseline before drawing a conclusion about movement.

How I worked through it. [edit]

Let me walk you through how I approached this. I started with the retention goal, then looked for a reader problem that could plausibly affect it. Continue reading was the proposed solution I needed to examine, and the brief left me with questions I could not answer on my own.

1. Start with the outcome, then question the evidence.

The assignment asks for an increase in the share of new app installs that return within seven days. The starting return figures are 22% on Android and 28% on iOS. I also see that 41% of Android sessions and 38% of iOS sessions end partway through an article, while push opt-in is 61% on Android and 34% on iOS. My initial instinct was to connect the first two observations. If readers leave articles unfinished, a way to continue might give some of them a reason to come back.

I had to slow that down. As the evidence section explains, an incomplete session can reflect an interruption, a completed task, or a deliberate stop. It tells me what happened in a session, not whether the reader wanted to return. The possible connection was a hypothesis to investigate, and I needed a more specific problem before committing to a solution.

The Android install trend required the same care. Because the analyst identified a tracking change, I would not compare the recent figure directly with the previous month or use that comparison to size the opportunity. The measurement caveat belongs in the decision from the beginning.

2. Find the actual user problem.

The support feedback about losing article position when the app is backgrounded stood out because it describes a concrete failure. In that scenario, a reader leaves an article, comes back later, and has to find the article again and work out where they were. I wanted to investigate that friction and its possible connection to the return outcome.

This is a more grounded starting point than treating every incomplete session as an engagement opportunity. The proposed audience is new app readers who interrupt an article and might benefit from a way back to it. I do not know how large that group is or how many want to resume. These are signals supplied in the assignment. I have not interviewed readers, reviewed additional research, or validated the problem with users.

Reading continuity: the product question

Is the problem that readers need another reason to open Wikipedia, or that the app makes it unnecessarily difficult to continue something they already started?

The support report made the second question more actionable. Reading continuity became the product concept I would test, with Continue reading as the proposed user-facing feature. The hypothesis is that reducing the effort of recovery could help some new readers return, while leaving their choice to move on intact.

Reader journey and target segment

The journey below isolates the handoff this proposal addresses. Discovery and longer reading stay outside the first release.

Journey stageReader's likely jobPotential friction

ReturnContinue the earlier reading or find it againReader may need to relocate the article and reconstruct where they were
Resume or move onContinue, dismiss, or finishFeature must preserve reader choice and make dismissal simple

The design constraint is reader choice. Continuing should be easy, and moving on should be equally easy.

3. Consider the possible solutions.

With that journey in mind, I compared three approaches. I wanted to see which part of the return path each one could repair, and what would still be left for the reader to do.

Alternatives considered

ApproachWhat it could solveWhat it leaves unresolved
1. Restore the last position automaticallyAddresses the reported loss of place when a reader backgrounds an articleImmediate recovery alone does not make an older interrupted article easy to find after the reader has left or opened other content
2. Surface interrupted articles on HomeCreates a visible return path without requiring a notification or accountNeeds reliable position saving and restoration. A list without persistence would solve only the findability part of the problem
3. Send a reminder notificationCould bring a reader back after they leave the appIntent to return is unknown, the app review reports notification fatigue, and Android push setup already exists. Android caps non-essential notifications at one per user per week

Local position saving and a visible Home entry point work together because they address different parts of the reported friction. One remembers where the reader stopped, and the other makes the article findable when the reader returns. That is the first test I would choose for this problem. Notifications would add a separate attention and delivery decision before I know whether the in-product path is useful, so I would defer them.

No comparative user research or feature-effect data was supplied. This is a reasoned comparison of options, with the combined approach still requiring validation.

4. Make the scope fit the actual constraints.

Once I had a candidate return path, the release window forced the scope to become specific. The first release needs to ship within six weeks. Cross-device synchronization requires account work estimated to take at least a quarter, and the designer is unavailable for the first two weeks. The assignment describes local position saving as easy on iOS. Android feasibility and shared behavior still need engineering confirmation, so I would write the behavior requirements without assuming implementation parity.

The proposed first release would save article position locally and show up to three recently updated interrupted articles on Home. Each entry would display the article title and approximate reading progress. Readers could resume at the saved position, or at the closest valid position if the article had changed. They could dismiss an individual entry or clear all saved positions after confirmation. Completed articles would be removed, and the section would be hidden when no eligible saved positions exist.

Three is a provisional display limit chosen to keep the Home surface compact. It is not a research-backed optimum, and I would review it with design and engineering. Cross-device synchronization waits on the account work. Push notifications stay out for the reasons described in the alternatives discussion. Reading streaks and streak-based social sharing cards add engagement mechanics, while personalized recommendations, AI summaries, and extra prompts introduce new behavior and data questions before the basic return path has been tested.

5. Take the privacy concern seriously.

The volunteer editor’s question about whether the app stores what they read, and where that information goes, changes how I think about the feature. Reading history can reveal sensitive interests. This is a product decision about the relationship I am asking readers to have with the app, as well as a technical and compliance question.

My proposal is local-first. It would require no account, keep saved positions on the device, and exclude article titles, URLs, stable article identifiers, and positions from analytics and server-side events. Readers could dismiss individual entries or clear all saved positions after confirmation. I would write those expectations into the acceptance criteria and the explanation shown to readers.

Aggregate feature-use events are different from article-level reading history. An event could record that a Continue reading section was shown or that a restoration succeeded, along with the experiment context needed for analysis, while excluding article titles, URLs, stable article identifiers, and positions. Aggregating those events would measure feature use without constructing a record of what a reader read or where they stopped. The proposed event schema needs engineering and privacy review so measurement does not quietly recreate the history the feature promises to protect.

These are proposed boundaries, not verified implementation details. Engineering and privacy still need to examine local storage behavior, backups, logs, crash reporting, deletion behavior, retention and expiry, and analytics pathways. Until that review is complete, I would not claim that all data is guaranteed to remain on-device. If the actual pathways cannot support the boundary, I would narrow the feature or pause the release.

6. Separate shipping the feature from proving it works.

Shipping Continue reading would not prove that it helped the retention goal. People might use it and still return at the same rate, or the people who use it might have returned anyway. Adoption is useful for understanding the experience, but it cannot stand in for the outcome.

I would propose a randomized holdout experiment for eligible new installs, with a 50/50 treatment and holdout split, intention-to-treat analysis, and results reported separately for Android and iOS. The current baselines are 22% on Android and 28% on iOS. My proposed decision threshold is at least a two-percentage-point absolute improvement over the holdout on each platform. That is a proposed decision rule for this exercise, not a forecast or a statistically validated target.

The analyst still needs to confirm the metric definition and denominator, event instrumentation, sample size, statistical power, minimum detectable effect, and experiment duration. The final cohort also needs a full seven-day maturation period after entering the experiment. A six-week implementation and rollout can end before that period completes, so the week-six readout may be immature or underpowered. I would report that result as inconclusive and continue measurement.

A positive result would show the proposed improvement against the holdout on each platform with acceptable guardrails, and would support a rollout discussion. An inconclusive result would leave the effect unresolved because of power, maturity, or measurement limitations. Low adoption would send me first to eligibility, discoverability, restoration reliability, and the question of whether the proposed audience actually has this need. Adoption without a measurable seven-day return improvement would make me revisit the reader problem and return path. If privacy, stability, or reader-trust guardrails deteriorate, I would pause or narrow the release and investigate before pushing for adoption.

Decision trail

This compact record separates what came from the brief, what I am proposing, and what still needs validation.

  1. Observed in the brief: the retention and incomplete-session figures, the support report about losing place, the notification complaint, and the volunteer editor’s privacy question.
  2. Chosen as the problem to investigate: the effort of finding an interrupted article again and recovering a useful reading position.
  3. Proposed first release: local position saving paired with a small Continue reading section on Home, with resume, dismissal, and history controls.
  4. Deferred: cross-device sync, push, reading streaks, streak-based social sharing cards, and personalized recommendations until the basic return path and its data boundaries are better understood.
  5. Still unresolved: the size of the reader need, implementation and privacy details, and whether a powered experiment shows a retention effect.

Nothing in this record claims that the feature has been validated with readers or shipped by a team.

How we would measure it [edit]

The measurement plan keeps the retention outcome separate from feature usage. The event definitions below prevent a saved position or a successful restoration from being mistaken for a seven-day return.

An incomplete article session is the observed session event. An eligible interrupted article is a proposed candidate for Home under the display rules. A saved local reading position is the stored anchor. A successful restoration returns the reader to that anchor, or to the closest valid position if the content has changed. A seven-day return is the primary outcome. These definitions need separate instrumentation.

Reading the result: Adoption is diagnostic, while seven-day return is the outcome. The walkthrough describes how I would respond to each result, including low adoption and deteriorating privacy, stability, or reader-trust guardrails. The final cohort must mature for seven days before its result is treated as complete.

What I would do in the first week [edit]

If I joined the team, I would use the first week to reduce uncertainty before committing to implementation. These are proposed actions, not conversations or approvals that have already happened.

  1. Start with the analyst. I would validate the retention metric, its denominator and instrumentation, the feasibility of a randomized holdout, and the Android tracking caveat. I would also ask what sample size, power, minimum detectable effect, and duration would make the proposed threshold interpretable.
  2. Work through the implementation with Engineering. I would confirm how local storage behaves, when position is saved, how restoration works after navigation or content changes, and what deletion really removes. The same review would cover Android feasibility, shared behavior, backups, logs, crash reporting, analytics pathways, and the privacy implications of each.
  3. Prepare the design work for the designer’s return. I would bring the key Home and reading states: an interrupted article, a saved position, an empty section, a completed article, dismissal, cleared history, and a restoration failure. When the designer returns, we would establish the interaction and accessibility details together.
  4. Review the proposal with the relevant team members. I would walk through the scope, acceptance criteria, measurement plan, and open questions with Engineering, Design, Analytics, and Privacy so that the first release reflects what the team can actually build and evaluate.
  5. End with a short list of decisions. I would separate what is ready to build from what remains unresolved, then bring the most consequential choices to the Senior Director: the privacy boundary, the experiment’s feasibility, any scope trade-off, and anything that could prevent a responsible six-week release.

AI use and authorship [edit]

I tried to keep the reasoning inspectable: start with the outcome, separate observation from interpretation, compare options against constraints and trust, and state what would change my mind. The walkthrough carries the narrative, while the tables keep the requirements and open questions available for review.

I used ChatGPT as an analysis and writing aid. It helped organize the supplied brief, challenge assumptions, pressure-test trade-offs, and refine requirements and prose. It did not provide user research, stakeholder conversations, engineering confirmation, approvals, or experimental results. I reviewed the metrics against the assignment and selected the final problem framing, scope, privacy boundaries, measurement plan, and recommendations.

The narrow first release follows from the specific reader problem I chose to investigate: helping someone recover an article and a useful reading position after an interruption. Local saving and a small, dismissible Continue reading section give that reader a way back while keeping control in their hands. The holdout experiment will test whether that convenience improves seven-day return on each platform. Before implementation and rollout, I would resolve Android behavior, privacy pathways, interaction details, and the experiment’s definitions and power. Those checks will determine whether this remains a responsible proposal to ship.
