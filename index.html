WIKIPEDIA APPS / READING CONTINUITY

Thread

Reading continuity in the Wikipedia apps
ArticleDiscussionReadView source

Help people pick up where curiosity left off.

This journal uses Thread as a proposed internal codename for this independent decision record. It is not an approved internal project, a Wikimedia product, or an official Wikimedia initiative. The product concept is Reading continuity in the Wikipedia apps. The user-facing feature name remains “Continue reading.” This journal documents the evidence, assumptions, trade-offs, and proposed decisions behind a six-week first release.

Scope of this document: The assignment scenario and its figures are fictional. The supplied brief is the source for the facts and metrics below. The interpretation, hypothesis, and proposed decisions are mine. This journal is a companion to, not a substitute for, the four interview deliverables: (A) a scope and measurement plan, (B) a Phabricator epic and two user story tasks, (C) a Slack update to the Senior Director, and (D) a public reply to the volunteer editor.

The assignment [edit]

The quarterly goal is to increase the share of new app installs that return within seven days. Leadership is interested in a “Continue reading” feature, with a first version expected within six weeks.

The PM owns scope, delivery, and updates to the Senior Director while the manager is away. The exercise asks for a scope and measurement plan, a Phabricator epic and two complete stories, a leadership update, and a public reply to a volunteer editor.

Support separately reports that the app loses a reader’s place when backgrounded. An app review complains about notification volume. A volunteer editor asks whether the app will store what people read and where that data goes.

The analyst warns that the Android drop in new installs last month is partly due to a tracking change. Direct month-over-month comparison is therefore not appropriate.

Reading continuity: the product question [edit]

Is the problem that readers need another reason to open Wikipedia, or that the app makes it unnecessarily difficult to continue something they already started?

I recommend testing Reading continuity before adding another engagement mechanic. That judgment starts with the reported loss of reading position, not with an assumption that every interrupted reader wants a reminder.

Reader journey and target segment [edit]

The proposed audience is not every new reader, and it is not everyone whose session ends mid-article. It is new app readers who interrupt an article and would benefit from being able to return to it. The brief does not establish how many readers fit that description or how many want to resume. That is the central user hypothesis to test, not a fact to assume.

Journey stageReader's likely jobPotential friction
DiscoverFind an answer or follow a questionNot the focus of this intervention
Open and readUnderstand an article at their own paceArticle experience must remain useful and stable
InterruptLeave temporarily, switch tasks, or stopApp may lose the current position when backgrounded
ReturnContinue the earlier reading or find it againReader may need to relocate the article and reconstruct where they were
Resume or move onContinue, dismiss, or finishFeature must preserve reader choice and make dismissal simple

The intervention targets the handoff between interruption and return. It does not try to solve discovery, create a reason to read longer, or treat leaving as a negative action. This is a narrower and more testable problem than “increase engagement.”

Alternatives considered [edit]

I compared three distinct approaches against the quarterly outcome, evidence in the brief, delivery constraints, and reader trust before selecting a first-release recommendation.

ApproachPotential valueWhy it was not the first-release choice
1. Restore the last position automaticallyFixes the reported loss of place when a reader backgrounds an articleUseful for immediate recovery, but alone it does not make an older interrupted article easy to find after the reader has left or opened other content
2. Surface interrupted articles on HomeCreates a visible return path without requiring a notification or accountNeeds reliable saved position and a way to restore it. A list without persistence would only solve part of the problem
3. Send a reminder notificationCould bring a reader back after they leave the appIntent to return is unknown, notification fatigue is already reported, and Android limits non-essential notifications to one per user per week

Recommendation: I recommend pairing local position persistence with a small Home entry point. Together they form one return path: remember where the reader stopped, then make that article findable when they come back. This addresses the reported friction more directly than a reminder, fits the six-week constraint better than sync, and preserves the reader's choice about whether to resume. The brief does not establish that this approach will improve seven-day return, so I would test the recommendation rather than treat it as a proven solution.

The alternatives are product approaches, not experimentally validated solutions. The brief provides no comparative user research or feature-effect data. The choice is a reasoned starting point to test.

Ecosystem and leverage [edit]

The feature sits inside a wider system. I would judge the recommendation by whether it creates value for readers without hidden costs for people who maintain, support, or trust Wikipedia.

Within the six-week constraint, I see the in-app return path as the most actionable opportunity. It responds to a support-reported usability issue, avoids account infrastructure, and can be tested against the stated outcome. This does not establish that Reading continuity is the largest possible growth opportunity across Wikipedia. It is a plausible opportunity that fits the evidence and constraints in this brief.

The trade-offs [edit]

1. Incomplete does not mean unfinished

A session ending partway through an article is an observable event, not proof that the reader wanted to return. Some people got what they needed. Others were interrupted. The feature should help the second group without treating every exit as a failure.

My recommendation

Use interruption as an eligibility signal, not as evidence of intent or a reason to send a prompt.

2. Retention versus trust

Reading history can make continuity useful, but it can also feel like surveillance. The volunteer editor’s question makes this a product requirement, not a footer disclaimer.

My proposed data boundary

Keep saved positions on-device and require no account. Send no article title, article identifier, or saved position to analytics or servers. Use only the minimum event data needed to evaluate the experiment and feature reliability, then verify logs, crash reporting, storage, and deletion with Engineering and privacy before release.

3. Cross-device convenience versus six-week feasibility

iOS local persistence is described as easy. Sync requires account work and at least a quarter. A cross-device experience may be more complete, but it does not fit the stated release window.

My recommendation

Start local-first. Treat sync as a later hypothesis to revisit if readers value continuity beyond one device.

4. More prompts versus notification fatigue

Push opt-in is higher on Android than iOS, but that does not establish that notifications are the right intervention. The app review explicitly complains about too many notifications, and Android has a one-per-week cap for non-essential notifications.

My recommendation

Use an in-product return path first. Do not spend the notification budget to solve a problem that may be addressed in the app.

5. Marketing visibility versus product focus

A reading-streak card could be campaign-friendly, but it introduces a social engagement mechanic and may increase pressure to collect or expose reading behavior.

My recommendation

Exclude it from the first release and offer a discovery-led campaign using existing sharing capabilities.

Proposed first-release scope [edit]

Recommendation: I recommend saving interrupted reading positions on-device, surfacing up to three recent articles on Home, and letting readers resume, dismiss, or clear their history.

The three-article limit is a compact-surface starting point, not a research-backed optimum. Validate findability and the limit in design review.

Included in the first releaseDeferred
  • Local position persistence
  • Continue reading on Home
  • Resume at saved position
  • Dismiss one article
  • Clear all reading history
  • Remove completed articles
  • Minimal experiment and reliability events, with no article title, identifier, or saved position
  • Cross-device sync
  • Push notifications
  • Reading streaks or social cards
  • Personalized recommendations
  • AI summaries or extra prompts

Help people pick up their curiosity where they left it. Do not turn curiosity into a retention mechanic.

How I would evaluate the proposal [edit]

Primary outcome: the existing share of new app installs that return within seven days. I would preserve the established event and denominator definition, randomize eligible new installs 50/50 to treatment and holdout, analyze intention-to-treat, and report Android and iOS separately.

Proposed decision threshold: at least +2 percentage points absolute lift versus holdout on each platform. This is a decision threshold for the exercise, not a forecast. Validate power, minimum detectable effect, sample size, and duration with an analyst before interpreting the result.

Guardrails: app stability, article-load success, restoration success, uninstalls, app reviews, support contacts, and privacy complaints. Feature adoption is diagnostic, not the outcome.

Decision rule and measurement caution: Do not compare Android directly with the previous month. The brief attributes part of the Android decline to a tracking change. If a powered test clears the proposed threshold on each platform and guardrails remain acceptable, I would recommend continued investment. If it does not, revisit the user problem and the return path rather than adding prompts by default. If the six-week point arrives before the experiment is adequately powered, report the result as inconclusive and continue measurement. Pause earlier if privacy, stability, or restoration guardrails materially worsen.

Decision trail [edit]

This section records how the recommendation follows from the brief. It is a decision record, not a claim that the proposed feature has been validated with readers or shipped by a team.

  1. Start with supplied signals. The brief reports that 38% of iOS sessions and 41% of Android sessions end partway through an article. Support separately reports that the app can lose a reader's place when backgrounded. These signals describe behavior and friction, but do not show how many readers want to return.
  2. Separate observation from interpretation. I do not treat an incomplete session as evidence of unmet need by itself. The actionable problem in the brief is the reported loss of place and the effort required to find an interrupted article again.
  3. Make a bounded hypothesis. A local saved position plus a modest Home entry point may reduce that effort and contribute to the existing seven-day return goal without adding notifications or requiring an account.
  4. Choose a first release that can test the hypothesis. I recommend pairing on-device position persistence with a return path, then letting readers resume, dismiss, or clear saved items. I would defer sync and reminders because they add delivery or trust costs before intent is established.
  5. Say what would change the recommendation. I would revisit the problem and return path if a powered experiment misses the proposed threshold or discovery finds little unmet need among interrupted readers. If the privacy boundary or reliability guardrails cannot be met, I would narrow or stop the release. An underpowered six-week result is inconclusive, not proof of success or failure.

Evidence still missing: reader interviews, segment size, intent to resume, comparative solution evidence, and experiment power were not supplied. I would validate these points in the first week. They remain open rather than being filled with invented findings.

Working method [edit]

  1. I start with the stated outcome, not the feature request.
  2. I map the reader journey before choosing an intervention.
  3. I separate supplied evidence from interpretation, assumptions, and hypotheses.
  4. I compare distinct approaches, then choose against impact, feasibility, and trust.
  5. I use constraints to narrow the first release, not lower the quality bar.
  6. I make privacy and reader control part of the experience definition.
  7. I specify behavior clearly and leave platform implementation to Engineering.
  8. I define what result would change my next decision.
