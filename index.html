WIKIPEDIA APPS / READING CONTINUITY

Reading continuity in the Wikipedia apps
ArticleDiscussionReadView source

A walkthrough of the evidence, questions, and trade-offs behind a proposed Continue reading feature.

This journal uses Thread as a proposed internal codename for this independent decision record. It is not an approved internal project, a Wikimedia product, or an official Wikimedia initiative. The product concept is Reading continuity in the Wikipedia apps. The user-facing feature name remains “Continue reading.” This journal documents the evidence, assumptions, trade-offs, and proposed decisions behind a six-week first release.

Scope of this document: The assignment scenario and its figures are fictional. The supplied brief is the source for the facts and metrics below. The interpretation, hypothesis, and proposed decisions are mine. This journal is a companion to, not a substitute for, the four interview deliverables: (A) a scope and measurement plan, (B) a Phabricator epic and two user story tasks, (C) a Slack update to the Senior Director, and (D) a public reply to the volunteer editor.

How I worked through it. [edit]

Let me walk you through how I approached this. The first thing I noticed was how easy it would be to jump from the retention goal to a feature.

1. Start with the outcome, then question the evidence.

The assignment asks for an increase in the share of new app installs that return within seven days. The starting figures are 22% on Android and 28% on iOS. I also see that 41% of Android sessions and 38% of iOS sessions end partway through an article. My initial instinct was to connect those observations: if readers are leaving articles unfinished, perhaps a way to continue would give them a reason to come back.

I had to slow that down. Someone who stops partway through may have found the answer they needed, been interrupted, or chosen to stop for the moment. The event tells me what happened in a session; it does not tell me whether the reader wanted to return. I would treat the possible connection as a hypothesis and look for a more specific problem before committing to a solution.

There is a measurement caveat as well. The analyst says that Android’s recent install performance was partly affected by a tracking change. Comparing it directly with the previous month would mix a change in measurement with a change in reader behavior. I would want a clean baseline before drawing any conclusion about movement.

2. Find the actual user problem.

The support feedback about losing a reader’s place when the app is backgrounded stood out because it describes a concrete failure. A reader leaves an article, comes back later, and has to find the article again and work out where they were. That is something I can investigate in the product and potentially improve.

It gives me a more grounded starting point than treating every incomplete session as an engagement opportunity. The proposed audience is new app readers who interrupt an article and might benefit from a way back to it. I do not know how large that group is or how many want to resume. These are signals supplied in the assignment; I have not interviewed readers, reviewed additional research, or validated the problem with users. I would make that uncertainty explicit in the first-week plan.

3. Consider the possible solutions.

With that problem in mind, I compared three approaches. Automatically restoring the last reading position would address the immediate loss of place when someone returns to the same article. It would not help much if the reader has opened other content or cannot find the earlier article.

A visible Continue reading section on Home could provide that missing return path without requiring an account or a notification. Its weakness is that the list depends on reliable position saving and sensible restoration. A visible list that returns someone to the wrong place would make the experience worse.

Push notifications could reach readers after they leave, but they assume an intent to return that the brief does not establish. The app review already complains about notification volume, and Android permits only one non-essential notification per user per week. I would be cautious about spending that limited opportunity on an unproven prompt.

That is why I favor combining local position saving with a small Home entry point. One remembers the place; the other makes the article findable. It is a coherent return path to test, while the evidence that it will improve seven-day return is still missing.

4. Make the scope fit the actual constraints.

The six-week release window then forces the scope to be honest about what can be built well. Cross-device synchronization needs account work that engineering estimates will take at least a quarter. The designer is unavailable for the first two weeks, so I would avoid making the first release depend on a broad new visual system.

I would save the reading position locally and show up to three recently interrupted articles on Home. Each item would include the article title and approximate reading progress. Readers could resume, dismiss an individual item, or clear their history. Completed articles would leave the section, and the section would be hidden when there is nothing to show.

The three-item limit is a practical starting decision for a compact Home surface, not a limit established by research. I would review it with design and engineering rather than present it as an optimum. Cross-device sync, push notifications, reading streaks, social sharing cards, and personalized recommendations stay outside this release because they add account, delivery, attention, or data complexity before the basic return path has been tested.

5. Take the privacy concern seriously.

The volunteer editor’s question about whether the app stores what they read, and where that information goes, changes how I think about the feature. Reading history can reveal sensitive interests. A convenience feature should not quietly become a reading surveillance system.

My proposal is local-first: no account requirement, positions stored on the device, no article-level reading history sent to analytics, and clear controls to dismiss entries or clear history. I would write those expectations into the acceptance criteria and the explanation shown to readers.

That is a proposed boundary, not a verified implementation. Engineering and privacy still need to examine storage, backups, crash reporting, logs, deletion behavior, and analytics pathways. Until that review is complete, I would not promise that data never leaves the device. If the actual pathways cannot support the boundary, I would narrow the feature or pause the release.

6. Separate shipping the feature from proving it works.

Shipping Continue reading would not prove that it helped the retention goal. People might use it and still return at the same rate, or the people who use it might have returned anyway. Adoption is useful for understanding the experience, but it cannot stand in for the outcome.

I would propose a randomized holdout experiment for eligible new installs, with a 50/50 treatment and holdout split and results reported separately for Android and iOS. The current baselines are 22% and 28%. My proposed decision threshold is a two-percentage-point absolute improvement against the holdout on each platform. That is a decision rule for this exercise, not a forecast or a statistically validated target.

The analyst would need to confirm instrumentation, sample size, power, minimum detectable effect, and experiment duration before I interpreted the result. If adoption is strong but retention does not improve, I would revisit the user problem and return path rather than add prompts. If adoption is low, I would first look at eligibility, discoverability, and restoration reliability, and whether the proposed audience actually has this need. If privacy or stability guardrails deteriorate, I would pause or narrow the release and investigate before pushing for adoption.

The assignment [edit]

The quarterly goal is to increase the share of new app installs that return within seven days. Leadership is interested in a “Continue reading” feature, with a first version expected within six weeks.

The PM owns scope, delivery, and updates to the Senior Director while the manager is away. The exercise asks for a scope and measurement plan, a Phabricator epic and two complete stories, a leadership update, and a public reply to a volunteer editor.

Support separately reports that the app loses a reader’s place when backgrounded. An app review complains about notification volume. A volunteer editor asks whether the app will store what people read and where that data goes.

The analyst warns that the Android drop in new installs last month is partly due to a tracking change. Direct month-over-month comparison is therefore not appropriate.

Reading continuity: the product question [edit]

Is the problem that readers need another reason to open Wikipedia, or that the app makes it unnecessarily difficult to continue something they already started?

I recommend testing Reading continuity before adding another engagement mechanic. That judgment starts with the reported loss of reading position, not with an assumption that every interrupted reader wants a reminder.

Reader journey and target segment [edit]

The journey below isolates the handoff this proposal addresses. It leaves discovery and longer reading outside the first release.

Journey stageReader's likely jobPotential friction
DiscoverFind an answer or follow a questionNot the focus of this intervention
Open and readUnderstand an article at their own paceArticle experience must remain useful and stable
InterruptLeave temporarily, switch tasks, or stopApp may lose the current position when backgrounded
ReturnContinue the earlier reading or find it againReader may need to relocate the article and reconstruct where they were
Resume or move onContinue, dismiss, or finishFeature must preserve reader choice and make dismissal simple

The important design constraint is reader choice: continuing should be easy, and moving on should be equally easy.

Alternatives considered [edit]

The comparison below keeps the three options separate. The walkthrough explains the judgment; this table records the practical limitation of each approach.

ApproachPotential valueWhy it was not the first-release choice
1. Restore the last position automaticallyFixes the reported loss of place when a reader backgrounds an articleUseful for immediate recovery, but alone it does not make an older interrupted article easy to find after the reader has left or opened other content
2. Surface interrupted articles on HomeCreates a visible return path without requiring a notification or accountNeeds reliable saved position and a way to restore it. A list without persistence would only solve part of the problem

My first week [edit]

If I joined the team, I would use the first week to reduce uncertainty before committing to implementation. These are proposed actions, not conversations or approvals that have already happened.

  1. Start with the analyst. I would validate the retention metric, its denominator and instrumentation, the feasibility of a randomized holdout, and the Android tracking caveat. I would also ask what sample size, power, minimum detectable effect, and duration would make the proposed threshold interpretable.
  2. Work through the implementation with engineering. I would confirm how local storage behaves, when position is saved, how restoration works after navigation or content changes, and what deletion really removes. The same review would cover backups, logs, crash reporting, analytics pathways, and the privacy implications of each.
  3. Prepare the design work for the designer’s return. I would bring the key Home and reading states: an interrupted article, a saved position, an empty section, a completed article, dismissal, cleared history, and a restoration failure. When the designer returns, we would establish the interaction and accessibility details together.
  4. Review the proposal with the relevant team members. I would walk through the scope, acceptance criteria, measurement plan, and open questions with engineering, design, analytics, and privacy so that the first release reflects what the team can actually build and evaluate.
  5. End with a short list of decisions. I would separate what is ready to build from what remains unresolved, then bring the most consequential choices to the Senior Director: the privacy boundary, the experiment’s feasibility, any scope trade-off, and anything that could prevent a responsible six-week release.

Decision trail [edit]

This compact record separates what came from the brief, what I am proposing, and what still needs validation.

  1. Observed in the brief: the retention and incomplete-session figures, the support report about losing place, the notification complaint, and the volunteer editor’s privacy question.
  2. Chosen as the problem to investigate: the effort of finding an interrupted article again and recovering a useful reading position.
  3. Proposed first release: local position saving paired with a small Continue reading section on Home, with resume, dismissal, and history controls.
  4. Deferred: sync, push, streaks, social sharing cards, and personalized recommendations until the basic return path and its data boundaries are better understood.
  5. Still unresolved: the size of the reader need, implementation and privacy details, and whether a powered experiment shows a retention effect.

Nothing in this record claims that the feature has been validated with readers or shipped by a team.

Working method [edit]

I try to make the reasoning inspectable: start with the outcome, separate observation from interpretation, compare options against constraints and trust, and state what would change my mind. The walkthrough shows those choices in context; the tables keep the details available for review.

AI use disclosure: ChatGPT helped organize analysis, challenge assumptions, pressure-test trade-offs, and refine requirements. I reviewed and selected the final scope, prioritization, and recommendations.
